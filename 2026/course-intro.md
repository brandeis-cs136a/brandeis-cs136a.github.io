## Course Introduction and Learning Goals

This course explores the central question of automatic speech recognition: *what does it mean to statistically model speech*?
We begin with a compact tour of the mathematical foundations, from signals and probability to maximum likelihood and information theory, and how they shaped classical sequence models and max-ent classifiers.
We then pivot to the modern deep learning era, examining how neural architectures built from perceptrons, recurrence, convolution, and attention absorbed and unified the classical pipeline into end-to-end systems, and how today's state-of-the-art models are trained and fine-tuned.
The course culminates in a group final project in which teams design and build a working speech system.

As a result of taking this course and fully engaging with the material and required work, you will be able to:

* Explain the fundamental principles of statistics, probability, information theory, and linear algebra that underpin statistical models of speech, both classical and neural.
* Understand and analyze trending machine learning practices for speech, including audio feature extraction, neural architectures built on convolution and attention, and fine-tuning of pretrained models.
* Deploy, monitor, and troubleshoot model training on a remote GPU cluster, working proficiently in a Linux/Unix environment with the command line, job schedulers, and scripting.
* Critically evaluate speech systems using automatic metrics, understanding their limitations, normalization pitfalls, and implications for fairness across speakers and domains.
* Design, build, and present a working speech system as a team, using version control (`git`) and reproducible, well-documented code.
